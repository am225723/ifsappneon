Welcome to working with firefighters.

In IFS, firefighter parts often react quickly when something feels too painful, too intense, or too much.

They may distract, numb, escape, shut down, act impulsively, or create urgency.

This practice is not about shaming those parts.

It is about understanding what they are trying to protect.

Begin by settling into your body.

Let your breathing be natural.

Take a slow breath in.

And let it out.

Now gently ask, is there a reactive or urgent part of me that I can notice today?

This might be a part that wants to avoid.

A part that wants to numb.

A part that wants to distract.

A part that wants to lash out.

A part that wants to disappear.

A part that wants immediate relief.

Choose one firefighter part to focus on.

Only choose one that feels safe enough to notice right now.

Now ask.

How do I feel toward this part?

If you feel judgment, fear, anger, or shame toward it, that is understandable.

See if you can notice those reactions as parts two.

Now gently turn toward the firefighter part and say, I know you may be trying to help.

I am not here to shame you.

I want to understand what you are protecting.

Now ask.

What do you do when you take over?

Now ask, What are you trying to stop me from feeling?

And what are you afraid would happen if you did not act quickly?

Let the answers come slowly.

Now ask, how long have you been doing this job?

and what happened that made this job feel necessary.

You do not have to go into every memory.

Just notice what the part wants you to know.

Now see if you can appreciate the protective intention.

Even if the strategy has consequences.

Even if the strategy causes problems.

The part may be trying to keep you from unbearable pain.

You might say.

Thank you for trying to protect me.

I understand that you learned to act fast.

I want to help so you do not have to work so hard.

Now ask.

What would you need in order to trust me a little more?

Maybe the part needs proof, a plan, support, boundaries, reassurance, a different way to handle intensity.

Now ask.

Would you be willing to pause for just one breath before acting next time?

If the part says yes, thank it.

If it says no, ask what it would need first.

Now imagine offering this part a small alternative.

Not a command, just a possibility, maybe breathing, texting support, taking a walk, using the app, writing in virtual paper, naming the part, putting a hand on your chest.

Ask.

Which alternative feels least threatening?

Now thank this firefighter part.

Let it know you are beginning to understand.

Take a slow breath in.

And let it out.

Return to the room.

And remember, this part does not need to be defeated.

It needs to be understood, supported, and gradually relieved of its extreme job.

The firefighter is not the enemy.

It is often a tired guardian standing near an old fire.

With patience, trust, and support, it may not have to work so hard.
